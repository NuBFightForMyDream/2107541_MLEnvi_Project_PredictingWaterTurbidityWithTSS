Water quality × satellites × machine learning

Can satellites tell how muddy a lake is?

By Chatrphol Ovanonchai · 6730084521

Muddy water, measured as Total Suspended Solids (TSS), warns us about erosion, runoff and poor water quality. Measuring it means sending people out with bottles. This project trains a model to estimate TSS from Landsat satellite images instead, and tests it on lakes the model has never seen.

1 · The idea

Muddy water reflects more red light

Clear water absorbs most sunlight and looks dark from space. Suspended sediment scatters light back up, especially red and near-infrared. The satellite measures how much light comes back in six color bands, and the model learns that pattern.

2 · The data

Where the measurements come from

Each dot is one lake sampling site, colored by its typical measured TSS. One in five sites was hidden from the model during training and used only to test it, so the score reflects how it would do on a brand-new lake.

3 · The models

Four models compared, then the best one tuned

A simple straight-line model is the baseline. Random Forest, XGBoost and a small neural network can learn curved relationships. The best one (Random Forest) was then tuned with a randomized search of its settings.

Show all the numbers

Test = lakes never seen in training. CV = 5-fold cross-validation on training lakes, grouped by site. RMSE / MAE are on the log(1 + TSS) scale. Typical error = median factor between predicted and measured TSS.

4 · How close are the guesses?

Predicted vs measured, for lakes the model never saw

Every dot is one water sample. Dots on the dashed line are perfect predictions; dots inside the shaded band are within 2× of the measured value. Both axes use a log scale because TSS ranges from almost clear to extremely muddy.

5 · What the model looks at

The red-light signals matter most

How much each satellite signal contributes to the tuned Random Forest's decisions. The right column shows which way each signal pushes the prediction, which matches the physics of light in water.

6 · So what?

What this model is good for, and what it is not

Good for

Screening thousands of lakes at once for relative muddiness: which lakes are clear, which are turbid, and where to send field teams first.

Not good for (yet)

Exact numbers for compliance. Predictions are squeezed toward the middle: very clear water is over-estimated and very muddy water is under-estimated.

How to improve

Add information the satellite can't see: rainfall before the image, land use around the lake and lake depth. Correct for differences between Landsat 5, 7 and 8 sensors.

How it was built

  1. Clean: keep cloud-free water pixels with a TSS measurement
  2. Features: 6 color bands, band ratios, water indices, season
  3. Split by lake: 80% of sites to train, 20% to test
  4. Train & compare: 4 models, 5-fold grouped cross-validation
  5. Tune the best model, then test it once

Why split by lake?

The same lake is sampled many times. If samples from one lake ended up in both training and testing, the model could simply remember that lake and look better than it really is. Splitting by site makes the test honest.

Data: AquaSat (Ross et al., 2019), in-situ water quality matched to Landsat surface reflectance. Map outline: us-atlas. Models: scikit-learn & XGBoost.
